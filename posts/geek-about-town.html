Do not avert your eyes.

Being offered with due regard to geekery and essays delivered on October 5, 2026

The reason we are discussing this now, the injustice that has us all spinning, is not the sexual assault that Jacob Geller committed in 2016. That is the greatest of his transgressions, and it is plenty to reckon with on its own. It is horrible, and it is haunting, but it is not what we are dealing with now. The reason that we are having this conversation is not the wrong thing Jacob Geller did in 2016. It is the wrong thing he has been doing, constantly and pervasively, for the last ten years.

We are not victims of Geller’s assault. But all of us, even those who just watched his videos once in a while, have been lied to about something essential. We have not all been hurt in the same way, nor to the same extent, and none of us suffered even a fraction of what his victim in 2016 did. But that doesn’t mean we haven’t been harmed.

The Chappell Roan Digression

Being a consideration of geekery and essays submitted on March 20, 2024

While you listen, consider the DJ. Stand with me in a silent club. The lights are still up. There are people here, talking quietly at tables. Someone laughs. Beverages clink in emptying glasses. It is bright and the people do not like it. Even as someone who does not club, I know this is incorrect. A club like this is not the club.

Until the DJ. The lights dim at his arrival. The space becomes new. He—indulge me, the DJ we are imagining just now is Male—weaves together the myriad threads of instrumentation and rhythm to make music—but not just music. He makes the club. The heat haze of dancing bodies, the churning assemblage of appendages in motion. (The wiggling. The jiggling. My heartbeat.) The DJ creates the space in which we are all contained. He is the one who draws the astral selves from Dylan Mills and Kesha Sebert, who Hydes them away, leaving Ke$ha and Dizzee Rascal to flail in the numinous melee of the club. The DJ makes the club, and remakes the people within it.

So when Ke$ha calls out the DJ in the bridge of “TiK ToK,” to whom is she speaking? And how does she address him?

Now You’re Thinking With Labels!

Being offered with due regard to teaching submitted on February 13, 2019

Because at its worst, what we mean when we say “I don’t want to put labels on people” is “I haven’t actually stopped dating my ex.” It’s a relationship cliche that “I don’t want to put a label on this” is just another way of saying “I’m unwilling to commit,” or “I’m excluding myself from this label because I want the benefits it implies, but not the obligations.” When we refuse to add labels to who we are, we’re refusing to recognize any commonality—or at least, any nameable commonality—with anyone. When my son calls me “Dad,” he labels me. To deny the label is to deny something essential about who we are to one another.

OMC must be stopped!

Being offered with due regard to essays and geekery offered on March 23, 2017

Come with me this far, at least: March Fadness is in truth an essay made of essays, a great metaekphrasis using art and memory to heighten and sharpen one another’s mystery. March Fadness is rooted in a belief that art—even the one-hit wonders of the 90s—offers access to humanity.

These votes are immensely complex; if two of us vote for the same song, can we say with any certainty that those two votes mean the same thing? We cannot. Are people voting for the songs? For the videos? For the memories? For the essays? For the essayists? For the preservation of their own brackets? Or against any of those things, as I always root for whoever the Jets are playing? Or do all these strands braid around the maypole of a single radio button? Can we have a meaningful conversation about “which song is the best” if we are not in communion about what purpose art serves?

That last question is why Justin St. Germain—and, by extension, OMC’s “How Bizarre”—must be stopped.
